# Core Loop

_The clearest current expression of what the player does._

## Core Sentence

> I noticed the warning. I studied the place. I restored or altered the right thing. Night answered my choice. Dawn showed me what I learned.

This is the spine of Gloamstead. Combat, resources, exploration, art direction, and progression should support this loop rather than bury it.

## Moment-to-Moment Loop

1. Explore a ruined, bleak place in third person.
2. Notice a cryptic warning, environmental clue, or strange reaction from the Heart.
3. Study nearby structures, terrain, objects, sound, light, and placement.
4. Restore, alter, undo, or activate a meaningful fixed-location structure.
5. Use dusk to prepare around the interpretation.
6. Night reveals the result of what the player did, missed, misread, or neglected.
7. The player survives through understanding, simple combat pressure, cleansing, activation, or emergency restoration.
8. Dawn gives payoff: feedback, visible consequence, resource reward, world healing, or a clearer rule.

## First Night Minimum Teaching Goal

The first night does not need to teach the entire adaptive-night system. It must teach the basic grammar:

> What I did during the day mattered. What I prepared at dusk mattered. Night showed me the result.

The player should learn that paying attention to the environment is not optional flavor. It is how survival works.

## First Night Suggested Structure

1. The player accidentally awakens the Heart.
2. The Heart gives a simple cryptic fragment.
3. The environment contains obvious but still interpretable clues.
4. The player restores one fixed structure, likely a lantern or threshold object.
5. Dusk gives a short preparation window where the restored object visibly reacts.
6. Night tests the relationship between clue, restoration, and consequence.
7. Dawn makes the lesson legible.

## Whether Adaptation Belongs in the Tutorial

The tutorial should imply adaptation without fully explaining it.

Recommended approach:

- First night teaches visible cause and effect.
- Second or third night teaches that neglected or wrong restorations can change the pressure.
- Later nights reveal that the darkness responds to patterns in player behavior, not just isolated choices.

This avoids front-loading a complicated system while still establishing that the world is responsive.

## Dawn Payoff Ideas

Dawn should be more than a fade-to-day reset. It is the release valve and feedback screen expressed in the world.

Possible dawn payoffs:

- **World clarity:** fog thins around correctly restored places.
- **Structure response:** restored objects bloom, steady, crack, dim, or change state based on performance.
- **Heart response:** the Heart speaks one slightly clearer fragment or reacts emotionally.
- **Rule confirmation:** the environment shows why the warning mattered.
- **Resource reward:** the player receives a night-earned resource, with better understanding producing better or cleaner yield.
- **Access change:** a path, door, root, bridge, or ruin becomes reachable.
- **Memory reveal:** a small visual echo of what the place used to be appears.
- **Consequence scar:** failed or wrong preparation leaves a readable mark that can be repaired later.
- **Sanctuary warmth:** a local zone becomes safer, clearer, warmer, or more coherent.

## Dawn Design Rule

Every dawn should answer at least one of these:

1. What did the player understand correctly?
2. What did the player misunderstand?
3. What changed in the sanctuary?
4. What resource or opportunity was earned?
5. What does the player now know that they did not know before night?
