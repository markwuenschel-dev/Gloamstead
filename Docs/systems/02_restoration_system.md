# Restoration System

_Replacing generic base-building with ritualistic restoration._

## Purpose

Restoration is the main action of the game. It replaces survival crafting and tower-defense construction.

The player restores meaningful structures, objects, and places that alter the rules of the sanctuary and night consequences.

## Restoration vs Building

Building implies generic placement and expansion. Restoration implies:

- history
- ritual
- meaning
- place-specific context
- consequences
- symbolic effects

The game should use restoration as the dominant verb.

## Example Restoration Types

### Lanterns

Mechanical possibilities:

- reduce fog
- define safe paths
- weaken certain enemies
- reveal night routes

Symbolic role:

- memory of paths
- warmth against absence

Risk:

- attracts things that follow light

### Gardens

Mechanical possibilities:

- slow corruption
- stabilize restored zones
- generate dawn healing

Symbolic role:

- life returning to dead ground

Risk:

- vulnerable to rot or inversion

### Mirrors

Mechanical possibilities:

- reveal hidden enemies
- expose corruption
- redirect light
- create temporary shields or illusions

Symbolic role:

- truth, memory, self-knowledge

Risk:

- reflects fears or hostile copies

### Bells

Mechanical possibilities:

- alert the player
- call help
- repel or stun certain threats

Symbolic role:

- attention, invitation, warning

Risk:

- calls listeners from the dark

### Root/Walls/Boundaries

Mechanical possibilities:

- define inside/outside
- block movement
- shape corruption spread

Symbolic role:

- sanctuary boundary

Risk:

- overbuilding may choke growth or create internal pressure

## Design Rule

Every restoration should answer:

1. What does it do mechanically?
2. What does it mean symbolically?
3. What risk does misuse or overuse create?
4. What kind of warning might reference it?
5. What night consequence might test it?


## Current Restoration Direction

Restoration should happen primarily at **fixed locations** rather than through freeform base-building. The player discovers meaningful ruined structures and decides how to restore, alter, undo, or activate them.

This keeps restoration tied to place, history, ritual, and interpretation rather than turning the game into a construction sandbox.

## Altering and Undoing Restorations

The player should be able to alter or undo some restorations. This supports experimentation, correction, and consequence.

Alteration should not feel like swapping upgrades in a menu. It should feel like changing the meaning or state of a place.

Examples:

- a lantern can remember a path, guard a threshold, or attract things that follow light
- a mirror can reveal corruption, redirect light, or reflect something dangerous
- a garden can stabilize ground, feed the Heart, or become vulnerable to rot
- a bell can warn, repel, or invite listeners

## Wrong Restoration Consequences

Restoring the wrong thing, restoring too early, ignoring context, or altering a structure poorly can make the next night harder. This is allowed and desirable as long as the result is fair and readable.

Wrong restoration should create:

- a stronger pressure
- a different pressure
- a risk to a restored object
- corruption in an unexpected location
- a harder but understandable night

It should not create arbitrary punishment. The player should be able to understand the mistake through dawn feedback.

## Resource and Discovery Model

Restoration should be based on both discovery/action and resource expenditure.

The current direction:

- day exploration and puzzle interpretation reveal what can be restored or altered
- night success provides a baseline resource reward
- better interpretation may provide better, cleaner, or more efficient rewards
- the baseline night resource acts as a safety valve if the player misses a discovery path
- the baseline resource should not be strong enough to carry the player through the entire game without understanding the world

The player should not be hard-stuck because they failed one puzzle, but the intended path should still reward learning and interpretation.

## End-State Restoration Fantasy

If the player manages to restore everything important in the initial place, the result should not merely be a decorated base. The ruin should feel awakened.

Possible full-restoration outcomes:

- the sanctuary becomes a coherent awakened ruin
- restored structures connect into a larger pattern
- the Heart becomes more lucid or transformed
- a hidden function of the place activates
- the island/place reveals what it originally was
- the final night changes because the restored place can answer back


## Restoration Visual Rule

Restoration should not simply make objects cleaner, brighter, or newer.

Restoration should make places feel:

- warmer
- clearer
- more coherent
- more symbolically legible
- more trusted by the world

The strongest visual rule is:

> Restoration restores perceptual coherence.

Before restoration, a place may have broken silhouettes, uncertain depth, restless fog, weak contrast, and dead material response. After restoration, edges clarify, light behaves more predictably, materials regain warmth, and the object's symbolic purpose becomes readable.

## Shared Restoration State System

All major restoration assets should share a unified visual state model:

### Before

- cold palette
- cracked or weathered materials
- weak silhouette separation
- inactive ritual markings
- fog or corruption obscuring purpose
- material surfaces that feel dry, dead, or memory-worn

### During

- warmth appears along cracks, roots, seams, carvings, or ritual lines
- motes gather around meaningful edges
- fog pulls away or briefly reveals hidden structure
- sound and light confirm that the object is remembering its function
- the object becomes readable before it becomes beautiful

### After

- local value contrast improves
- shape language becomes stable
- warm amber, muted gold, or faint teal accents appear
- nearby ground, roots, moss, stone, or air show small signs of recovery
- the object feels active, sacred, and slightly vulnerable

## Visual Treatment by Restoration Type

### Lanterns

Lanterns should visually suggest memory, path, and warmth against absence. Their light should carve readable safe space out of fog. Restored lanterns may leave a faint after-glow on nearby stone or ground, implying that light remembers where it has been.

### Gardens

Gardens should not become cheerful decorations. They are fragile life returning to dead ground. Restoration may bring low moss, pale blooms, root-veins, wet soil, and subtle breath-like motion. Corrupted gardens should feel inverted: too still, too glossy, or quietly rotting from beneath.

### Mirrors

Mirrors should feel like truth and danger. Their surfaces can use liquid, stained-glass, mercury, or dark-water behaviors. A restored mirror should clarify hidden realities, but a misused or corrupted mirror may reflect hostile copies, false paths, or the player's mistakes.

### Bells

Bells should feel ceremonial rather than utilitarian. Their restoration can reintroduce resonance: visible vibration in dust, motes pushed by sound, fog rings, or distant answering chimes. Their risk is invitation — they call things as much as they repel them.

### Roots / Walls / Boundaries

Boundaries should feel alive, ritualized, and morally ambiguous. Restoring them defines inside and outside, but over-restoration may choke growth or create pressure. Their visual language should include root tension, stone seams, threshold markings, and subtle breathing movement.
